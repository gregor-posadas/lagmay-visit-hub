When the Waters Rise

About the event

Why we are bringing a scientist and two Berkeley scholars of the Philippines together to talk about floods, why here, who we are, and answers to common questions.

Why this conversation

Floods in the Philippines are usually told as an engineering story. Rain falls, rivers rise, and the fix is a higher wall or a deeper drain. That story is true, but it leaves out most of what decides who ends up under water.

Which towns get mapped, which get protected and which get concrete is settled by budgets, politics and choices that go back more than a century. In 2025 the country saw this plainly when billions of pesos meant for flood control went to projects that were poorly built or never built at all, and tens of thousands marched in protest. A flood is a natural hazard. Who it hurts is a human decision.

So this event puts three fields on one stage.

  • The science. Dr. Lagmay has spent his career showing where water will go, and making those maps free so that a mayor, a planner or a family can use them before the storm.
  • The state and its money. Dr. Claudio traces how the Philippine state was built to run on too little, and what that has meant for what it can build and maintain.
  • The built world. Dr. Martinez shows how American rule used concrete and city planning to remake the islands, and how those plans still shape who lives where the water comes.

None of these fields can explain the floods alone, and none can fix them alone. We want engineers to hear from history and political economy, those fields to hear from the scientist, and everyone in the room to leave with a fuller picture than any one of them could give.

Why here

The Bay Area is home to one of the largest Filipino communities in the United States, and many people here have family in towns that flood almost every year. For them, a typhoon is not a headline but a phone call home. We want this to be a place where that worry meets the people who study it, and where people with no ties to the Philippines can see why it matters to their neighbours.

Who is organizing it

We are three Filipino graduate students at UC Berkeley, each coming to floods from a different field. Gregor Posadas studies water governance in Civil and Environmental Engineering, and Noam Anglo is in Development EngineeringA Berkeley field that designs, tests and studies technology with and for communities in low-resource settings.. Together they work on BahaWatch, a low-cost flood-sensing project. Veronica Sison is a historian in Southeast Asian Studies whose research traces technocratic thoughtThe idea that trained experts, like engineers and economists, should make a country's big decisions. in the Philippines. At Stanford, Rapha Felipe leads the Stanford side of the visit, where Dr. Lagmay also plans to speak, and has helped find support for his trip. Before joining Stanford's Baker Lab, where she now works on disaster risk, she mapped flood risk across the Philippines at the Asian Infrastructure Investment BankA development bank, founded in 2016, that lends money for roads, power, water and flood protection across Asia..

Flooding in the Philippines

What is Project NOAH?

NOAH stands for Nationwide Operational Assessment of Hazards. It maps where floods, landslides and storm surges can strike, anywhere in the Philippines, and makes the maps free for anyone to use at noah.up.edu.ph. It was launched in 2012 under the Department of Science and Technology, with Dr. Lagmay as its head, and has been run by the University of the Philippines since 2017, as the flagship of the UP Resilience InstituteA University of the Philippines institute that studies disasters and climate risk and helps communities prepare., which Dr. Lagmay leads. The flood maps on our event page are NOAH's.

What is a "100-year flood"?

A flood so big that it has a 1 in 100 chance of happening in any given year. It does not mean once a century: two can come in back-to-back years. NOAH maps floods for rains of three sizes, with a 1 in 5, 1 in 25 and 1 in 100 chance each year. Our map uses the biggest, to show everywhere water could reach.

Why does the Philippines flood so often?

Several reasons pile up:

  • Storms. About 20 tropical cyclonesRotating storms that form over warm tropical seas, from tropical depressions to typhoons. PAGASA, the national weather service, names each one that enters Philippine waters. enter Philippine waters every year (source: PAGASA, Tropical cyclone information), and 8 or 9 cross the country.
  • The monsoon. From about June to September the habagatThe southwest monsoon: months of moist wind from the southwest that bring steady, heavy rain to the western Philippines, and much worse rain when a typhoon passes nearby. brings weeks of heavy rain, even without a typhoon.
  • Sinking land. Much of Metro Manila and the land around northern Manila Bay is sinking, largely because groundwater is pumped out faster than it returns. Between 2003 and 2011 parts of Caloocan, Malabon, Navotas and Valenzuela sank by up to 4.2 cm a year (source: Eco, Rodolfo, Sulapas et al., JSM Environmental Science and Ecology, 2020). In parts of Pampanga, researchers found neighbourhoods permanently under water (source: UP Resilience Institute, 2025).
  • Cities built for less rain. Metro Manila's drains handle about 30 mm of rain an hour (source: Inquirer, 25 Jul 2024); in July 2024 rain reached 74 mm an hour.
  • Who lives where. Many families, often the poorest, live along rivers, creeks and coasts, where water rises first.

The Philippines ranks first in the world for disaster risk in the WorldRiskReport 2026 (source: WorldRiskReport 2026, Bündnis Entwicklung Hilft and IFHV).

What was the flood-control scandal?

In July 2025 President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. condemned failed and "ghost" flood-control projects (source: Philstar, 28 Jul 2025), built badly or not at all though they were paid for. Hearings, resignations, an independent commission, mass protests and the first court cases followed. Those charged are presumed innocent until a court decides. The Flood history page has the timeline, with a source for every step.

Which floods will come up at the event?

The worst since 1991, from the Ormoc flash flood to Typhoon Tino in Cebu in 2025. The Flood history page tells each one, with photos.
