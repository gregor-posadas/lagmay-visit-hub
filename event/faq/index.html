The event

Everyone is welcome: students, faculty, staff and anyone from the wider Bay Area, whether your family is from the Philippines or you have never been. It's free.

Monday, November 9, 2026, from 3 to 5 PM Pacific. If anything changes, everyone who RSVPs hears first. It's in Banatao Auditorium, in Sutardja Dai Hall on the north side of the UC Berkeley campus; see how to get there.

Yes. The RSVP form has a place for questions, and we'll bring a selection to the conversation, along with questions from the room as time allows.

What is the morning lecture?

Earlier the same day, Dr. Lagmay gives a guest lecture in Development Engineering 203, Digital Transformation of Development, a graduate course at Berkeley. The afternoon conversation is the public event.

Why hold this in the Bay Area?

Many families here have roots in the provinces these floods hit. Every typhoon season, the calls home, the remittances and the balikbayan boxesBoxes of food, clothes and supplies that Filipinos abroad send home to family, usually by sea. already connect the two places. This conversation asks what more that connection can do, and how people with no ties to the Philippines can help too.

Flooding in the Philippines

What is Project NOAH?

NOAH stands for Nationwide Operational Assessment of Hazards. It maps where floods, landslides and storm surges can strike, anywhere in the Philippines, and makes the maps free for anyone to use at noah.up.edu.ph. It was launched in 2012 under the Department of Science and Technology, with Dr. Lagmay as its head, and has been run by the University of the Philippines since 2017, as the flagship of the UP Resilience InstituteA University of the Philippines institute that studies disasters and climate risk and helps communities prepare., which Dr. Lagmay leads. The flood maps on our event page are NOAH's.

What is a "100-year flood"?

A flood so big that it has a 1 in 100 chance of happening in any given year. It does not mean once a century: two can come in back-to-back years. NOAH maps floods for rains of three sizes, with a 1 in 5, 1 in 25 and 1 in 100 chance each year. Our map uses the biggest, to show everywhere water could reach.

Why does the Philippines flood so often?

Several reasons pile up:

  • Storms. About 20 tropical cyclonesRotating storms that form over warm tropical seas, from tropical depressions to typhoons. PAGASA, the national weather service, names each one that enters Philippine waters. enter Philippine waters every year (source: PAGASA, Tropical cyclone information), and 8 or 9 cross the country.
  • The monsoon. From about June to September the habagatThe southwest monsoon: months of moist wind from the southwest that bring steady, heavy rain to the western Philippines, and much worse rain when a typhoon passes nearby. brings weeks of heavy rain, even without a typhoon.
  • Sinking land. Much of Metro Manila and the land around northern Manila Bay is sinking, largely because groundwater is pumped out faster than it returns. Between 2003 and 2011 parts of Caloocan, Malabon, Navotas and Valenzuela sank by up to 4.2 cm a year (source: Eco, Rodolfo, Sulapas et al., JSM Environmental Science and Ecology, 2020). In parts of Pampanga, researchers found neighbourhoods permanently under water (source: UP Resilience Institute, 2025).
  • Cities built for less rain. Metro Manila's drains handle about 30 mm of rain an hour (source: Inquirer, 25 Jul 2024); in July 2024 rain reached 74 mm an hour.
  • Who lives where. Many families, often the poorest, live along rivers, creeks and coasts, where water rises first.

The Philippines ranks first in the world for disaster risk in the WorldRiskReport 2026 (source: WorldRiskReport 2026, Bündnis Entwicklung Hilft and IFHV).

What was the flood-control scandal?

In July 2025 President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. condemned failed and "ghost" flood-control projects (source: Philstar, 28 Jul 2025), built badly or not at all though they were paid for. Hearings, resignations, an independent commission, mass protests and the first court cases followed. Those charged are presumed innocent until a court decides. The Flood history page has the timeline, with a source for every step.

Which floods will come up at the event?

The worst since 1991, from the Ormoc flash flood to Typhoon Tino in Cebu in 2025. The Flood history page tells each one, with photos.

What happens to my RSVP answers?

Your name and email are only for the RSVP list; they never appear on the page or at the event. The map shows only counts, by Bay Area county and Philippine province. If you share a flood story and say yes to sharing it, a teammate reads it first, and it's never shown with a name.

Where do the maps come from?

The flood areas are UP NOAH's; the buildings come from Google, Microsoft and OpenStreetMap; the streets from OpenStreetMap. The full list, with links, is under Data and sources on the event page.
